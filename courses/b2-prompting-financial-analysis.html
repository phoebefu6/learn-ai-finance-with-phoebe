Part 1 · covers CFI advanced prompting + Coursera GenAI Essentials

Anatomy of a reliable finance prompt 6 min live

A strong finance prompt has six layers, and they stack. Drop one and the answer gets vaguer, more invented, or wrongly formatted. You do not need all six every time - but knowing the full stack means you know exactly which layer to add when an answer disappoints.

LiveThe six layers, in the order you write them3 min▶

Write them top to bottom and the prompt almost drafts itself. Each layer answers one question the model would otherwise guess at.

  • Role - "You are a controller / FP&A analyst / credit analyst." Sets vocabulary and rigour. A model told it is an analyst hedges and shows work; told nothing, it chats.
  • Context - the business, the period, the audience for the answer. "This is for a board pack" produces different prose than "explain to me".
  • Data - paste the actual numbers. This is rail-one grounding from b1. Public or synthetic data in a consumer tool; real financials in enterprise or local only.
  • Task - one clear verb: explain, compare, reconcile, draft. Two tasks in one prompt gives you two half-answers.
  • Format - table, bullets, JSON, word count. Say it up front and you stop re-asking "now put that in a table".
  • Constraints - the honesty rules: show every calculation, use only the numbers supplied, say "not in the data" instead of inventing, and state any assumption made.
The constraints layer is where finance lives Anyone can ask for a summary. The four constraint lines - show work, only supplied numbers, "not in the data", state assumptions - are what turn a chatty answer into one you can put your name on. Never skip them.
Self-studyThe constraint lines, ready to paste2 min read▶

Keep these four lines on a sticky note. They are the difference between an answer you trust and one you re-check line by line.

★ The reusable constraint blockRules: - Use ONLY the numbers I supplied above. Do not add outside figures. - Show every calculation step by step so I can check the math. - If something you need is missing, say "not in the data" - do not estimate. - State any assumption you make as a separate, labelled line.

Paste this at the end of any analytical prompt. It costs you four lines and buys you a checkable answer. Everything after this in the track assumes you are using something like it.

The core patterns - five moves that cover most work 6 min live

You do not need fifty prompt tricks. Five patterns handle the overwhelming majority of finance asks, and they combine. Learn when each one earns its place and you stop guessing.

LivePattern picker - when to reach for each3 min▶
PatternWhat it doesReach for it when...
Few-shot (show an example)Give one sample comment in your house style; the model matches itYou want consistent tone and structure across many outputs
Role prompting"You are a controller reviewing the close"You want the rigour and vocabulary of a specific finance role
Step-by-step decomposition"First compute variances, then rank, then comment on the top 3"The task has stages and you want the math visible and ordered
Output control"Return a 3-column table" or "return valid JSON with these keys"The answer feeds a doc, a sheet, or another system
Self-check"List your assumptions and what I should verify before I use this"Always - it is your built-in rail-two review step

Few-shot is the quiet hero. One good example of a variance comment teaches the model your style faster than three paragraphs of instructions ever will.

Self-studyFew-shot and self-check, shown2 min read▶

Few-shot means you supply the pattern, not just the instruction. Watch how one example locks the style:

★ Few-shot: teach the house style with one exampleHere is the style I want for variance comments: Example: "Marketing was 120 over budget (620 vs 500, +24%), driven by the Q2 campaign pulled forward from Q3. Timing, not overspend - full-year still on plan." Now write comments in that exact style for these three variances: [paste your variances]

Self-check makes the model hand you its own review notes - the single cheapest way to honour rail two inside the prompt itself:

★ Self-check: make it flag its own soft spotsAfter your analysis, add a short section titled "Verify before use": list every assumption you made, any number you were unsure about, and the one thing I should double-check against source before I send this.
Part 3 · finance-specific hygiene

Finance guardrails inside the prompt 3 min live

General prompting advice ignores the things that get finance people in trouble: the wrong currency, a quarter compared to a full year, or a percentage rounded three different ways in one memo. Bake the hygiene into the prompt so the model cannot drift.

LiveFive lines that stop silent finance errors2 min▶
  • Currency and units. "All figures in SGD thousands" - or the model may silently mix dollars and thousands, or assume USD.
  • Period labels. Spell out Q1 vs FY, actual vs budget, this year vs last. A comparison against the wrong base is worse than no comparison.
  • Rounding. "Round to whole numbers; percentages to one decimal." Consistency reads as competence in a board pack.
  • Materiality. "Only comment on variances above 50k or 10%." Stops the model narrating trivia and burying the real story.
  • Only these numbers. The b1 grounding rule, restated every time: no outside figures, no memory, no filling gaps.
Real world

The memo that mixed thousands and millions. An analyst pasted a P&L in thousands but asked for commentary "in millions" - the model gamely reported revenue of "4.1 million" when it was 4.1 thousand-times-something-else. It looked authoritative and was off by orders of magnitude. One line - "all figures in SGD thousands, do not convert" - would have prevented it. Guardrails are cheaper than corrections.

Rebuild "comment on these results" into a real prompt ★ 12 min · everyone builds

The most common finance prompt in the wild is also the worst: "comment on these results". We take that lazy ask, run it, feel the vagueness, then rebuild it layer by layer on a small supplied P&L - and iterate once to sharpen it.

Open ChatGPT or Claude. Paste the small P&L below (it is synthetic - safe for any tool, rail one). You will use it for both prompts.

Run the vague version first (Prompt A). Notice what you get: generic prose, maybe invented drivers, no math shown, no idea which variances actually matter.

Now rebuild it with the six layers (Prompt B): role, context, the data, one clear task, a table format, and the constraint block. Run it.

Compare. The structured answer ranks variances by size, shows the subtraction, respects your materiality threshold, and flags what to verify. That is the same fiction-to-checkable jump from b1, now on purpose.

Iterate once. Add a single refinement - "add a one-line 'so what' for the CFO under the table" - and re-run. Feel how cheap it is to steer once the structure is right. That is the whole skill.

Prompt A - vague (produces generic, ungrounded prose)Here are our results. Comment on these results. Revenue 4,120. Marketing 620. Salaries 1,900. Operating profit 700.
★ Prompt B - the six-layer rebuildYou are an FP&A analyst preparing month-end commentary for the CFO. Context: this is our May actual vs budget, SGD thousands, one business unit. Data (use ONLY these numbers): Line Actual Budget Revenue 4,120 3,950 Marketing 620 500 Salaries 1,900 1,880 Other opex 900 920 Operating profit 700 650 Task: explain what drove the operating-profit variance vs budget. Format: a table with columns Line | Actual | Budget | Variance | % | Comment, then one line "So what for the CFO" beneath it. Rules: - Use ONLY the numbers above; all figures SGD thousands; percentages to 1 decimal. - Only comment on variances above 50 or 10%. - Show the subtraction for each variance so I can check it. - If something needed is missing, say "not in the data" - do not estimate. - End with "Verify before use": list any assumption and what I should double-check.
Why we rebuild rather than nag When Prompt A disappoints, the instinct is to type "no, be more specific". That is slow and unreliable. Adding the missing layers is faster and repeatable - and it is exactly what the anatomy diagram is for. Diagnose the gap, add the layer.
Demo 2 of 2

Bottle it: a reusable variance-commentary template ★ 8 min · build your own

A great prompt you have to rewrite every month is a chore. A great prompt with placeholders is a tool. We turn Prompt B into a template you fill in seconds - the first entry in your prompt library.

Take the structure of Prompt B and replace the specifics with clearly marked placeholders in square brackets: role, period, currency, the data table, and the materiality threshold.

Save it somewhere you will find it - a notes doc, a shared team page, or your tool's saved-prompts feature. Give it a name: "Variance commentary v1".

Test the template by filling it with this month's numbers. If a placeholder is unclear, tighten the wording now, while you remember what you meant.

Start a running "prompt library" file. Every reusable prompt from this track goes in it - by b10 you will have a small, battle-tested toolkit no one handed you.

★ Variance commentary template (fill the [placeholders])You are a [FP&A analyst / controller] preparing [monthly / quarterly] commentary for [the CFO / the board]. All figures in [SGD thousands]. Period: [May actual vs budget]. Data (use ONLY these numbers): [paste your table: Line | Actual | Budget] Task: explain the drivers of the [operating-profit] variance vs [budget]. Format: a table with columns Line | Actual | Budget | Variance | % | Comment, then one line "So what for [the CFO]". Rules: - Use ONLY the numbers above; percentages to [1] decimal. - Only comment on variances above [50] or [10%]. - Show each subtraction so I can check the math. - Say "not in the data" for anything missing - do not estimate. - End with "Verify before use": assumptions + what I should double-check.
Real world

From two hours to twenty minutes. A group FP&A analyst templated her monthly variance pack this way. The first draft used to eat a morning; now she pastes the numbers, runs the template, and spends her time on the judgement - checking the math and sharpening the "so what". The AI did not replace her. It deleted the boring 80% so she could do the 20% that is actually her job.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Finish both demos if you ran short in class - especially the rebuild. Feeling the jump from Prompt A to Prompt B is the lesson.
  • Take one prompt you actually use at work and add the missing anatomy layers. Note which layer was missing - that is usually your habitual blind spot.
  • Build one more template from your real recurring work: a flash-report prompt, a reconciliation-explainer, or a KPI-summary. Add it to your prompt library.
  • Try the few-shot pattern once: give the model one example of your house style and see how much closer it lands than instructions alone.
  • Optional: swap templates with a colleague. The fastest way to improve a prompt is to watch someone else fill it in and get confused.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What are the six layers of a reliable finance prompt?

Role sets the standard, context fits your world, data grounds it, task is the one job, format shapes the output, and constraints carry the honesty rules. Drop one and the answer drifts.

2 · You need the same style of variance comment across twenty line items. Which pattern helps most?

One concrete example teaches style faster than any amount of instruction. Few-shot is the quiet workhorse of consistent finance output.

3 · Why add a self-check line ("list your assumptions and what I should verify")?

Self-check hands you the model's own soft spots to verify. It does not replace your review - it focuses it. That is rail two, baked into the prompt.
