Part 1 · covers CFI AI-enhanced analysis + Coursera GenAI Essentials

The killer use-case: numbers to narrative 6 min live

Survey after survey, the reporting write-up is the task finance teams most want to hand to AI - and the one where the payoff is fastest. The numbers are already done and checked; the model is only turning them into sentences. That framing matters: you are not asking it to know anything, you are asking it to phrase what you already know. The human owns the final word, always.

LiveFour write-ups, one move3 min▶

MD&A, variance commentary, the exec summary, and board-deck bullets look like four jobs. They are one move done at four lengths: take reconciled numbers, explain what changed, why, and what it means, in the right voice for the reader. AI drafts all four fast once you feed it the numbers and the structure. Your job shifts from typing to judging.

  • Variance commentary - the line-by-line story of actual vs plan/prior. Most repetitive, biggest time win.
  • MD&A - the management discussion around the statements. Longer, more standardised, style-sensitive.
  • Exec summary - the one-screen "so what" for leadership. Ruthless compression is the whole skill.
  • Board bullets - the deck version. Decision-relevant, no throat-clearing.
Why the payoff is real and safe The numbers are already reconciled, so there is no arithmetic to trust the model with. It is a pure language task over supplied data - the exact quadrant b1 said to trust. Low risk, high hours saved.

Techniques that make it board-grade 5 min live

A generic prompt gives you generic prose - the vague, padded paragraphs everyone recognises as "written by AI". Board-grade output comes from five deliberate moves. Each one trades a bit of prompt effort for a draft that needs far less editing and sounds like your firm, not a chatbot.

LiveThe five moves, and what each buys you3 min▶
TechniqueEffect on the draft
Feed reconciled numbers and last period's commentary as a style example (few-shot)Draft copies your house voice, structure, and length - not the model's default register
Enforce structure: what changed / why / outlookEvery point becomes decision-relevant; no wandering paragraphs
Apply a materiality filter (e.g. "only variances over $50k or 5%")The board sees the three things that matter, not fifteen that do not
Fix terminology up front (gross margin, run-rate, opex - your definitions)Consistent language across sections and across months
Strip AI "tells" - demand specificity from the actual numbersKills generic filler ("various factors", "market dynamics"); forces "$240k, driven by the freight rate step-up"

The last one matters most for credibility. AI filler is vague because the model is guessing at meaning. When you require every claim to cite a supplied figure, there is nothing left to pad with - the draft either ties to your numbers or it does not, and you can see which instantly.

Few-shot is your secret weapon Paste last quarter's commentary and say "match this voice, structure, and length, using this quarter's numbers". One good example teaches the model your style faster than a paragraph of instructions ever will.
Self-studySpotting AI "tells" before the board does2 min read▶

Reviewers smell AI prose instantly, and it costs you trust. Learn the tells so you can strip them on the edit pass:

  • Vague causation. "Driven by a combination of factors" - if it does not name the figure and the driver, cut or replace it.
  • Hedging everywhere. "May have contributed to a potential shift" - the board wants a view, not a weather forecast.
  • Symmetry padding. A tidy sentence for every line even when only three lines matter. Delete the filler; keep the material.
  • No "so what". It describes the movement but never says what leadership should do or watch. That missing sentence is your job.
Real world

The commentary that got sent back. An FP&A analyst shipped an AI first draft nearly unedited. The CFO returned it with one comment: "This says a lot and tells me nothing." The numbers were right; the narrative had no view. The fix took ten minutes of human editing - and became the standing rule: AI writes the draft, a human puts the spine in.

Part 3 · the reader lens that changes everything

Audience: write for the board 3 min live

The same numbers get written up completely differently for the board than for your finance peers. The board is time-poor, non-specialist on the detail, and there to decide. Write for them and the whole draft tightens.

LiveFour rules for board-facing narrative2 min▶
  • Concise. One screen, not three. If it does not change a decision, it does not make the cut.
  • Decision-relevant. Lead with what the board should notice, approve, or worry about - not a tour of every line.
  • Always the "so what". Every material point ends with the implication: what it means for the forecast, the covenant, the plan.
  • No jargon. "Run-rate opex" and "phasing" are fine internally; spell out the meaning for a mixed board. Clarity reads as competence.

Bake these into the prompt as instructions, and again into your edit pass. The model can follow "write for a time-poor board, lead with the so-what, no unexplained jargon" - but only you can judge whether it actually landed.

Numbers are made up and safe for any consumer tool (rail one).

Open any chat AI. Copy the variance bridge below into your prompt exactly - these are the only numbers the model may use.

Ask for commentary structured as what changed / why / outlook, with a materiality filter so only the big movers get a paragraph.

Read the draft against the numbers. Does every "why" cite a figure? Is anything invented or hedged? Flag the tells.

Edit it. Cut the filler, add the real driver where the model guessed, and write the one "so what" sentence the board needs on each material line.

★ The variance-commentary prompt (grounded)You are drafting the monthly variance commentary for a finance team. Use ONLY the numbers in this bridge (Actual vs Budget, $000s): Line Actual Budget Variance Revenue 4,180 4,000 +180 COGS 2,560 2,380 -180 Gross profit 1,620 1,620 0 Sales & mktg 620 560 -60 G&A 410 420 +10 EBITDA 590 640 -50 Write commentary structured as: WHAT CHANGED / WHY / OUTLOOK. Rules: only comment on variances of $40k or more (materiality filter); every "why" must cite a figure from the bridge; if a driver is not in the data, say "driver not in data - to confirm" rather than inventing one; plain language for a time-poor board; end each material line with the "so what".
The "driver not in data" trick A bridge shows the size of a variance, not its cause. Forcing the model to say "driver not in data - to confirm" turns invisible guesses into a checklist of things you go and confirm. That is grounding doing its job.

Exec summary from a management pack - then edit the filler out ★ 8 min · build your own

Now the compression skill. You get a small synthetic management pack, ask for a one-screen exec summary, then do the human edit pass that removes filler and fixes the "so what" - the 30% that turns a draft into something a CEO reads and acts on.

Paste the mini management pack below. Ask for an exec summary of no more than six bullets, each decision-relevant, no jargon.

Read the draft as the CEO would. Which bullets describe rather than decide? Which hedge? Which bury the "so what"?

Edit pass: delete any bullet that does not change a decision, rewrite vague causation with the actual figure, and add the implication to each survivor.

Sanity check the tone: would you say this out loud to the board in the meeting? If not, plainer words.

★ The exec-summary prompt (grounded)Write a board exec summary from ONLY this management pack (synthetic, $000s): Revenue 4,180 (budget 4,000, prior month 3,950); EBITDA 590 (budget 640). Cash 2,300, down 180 on the month (one-off tax payment 220). DSO 52 days, up from 46 - two large customers now paying at 75 days. Order book 6,100, up 12% on prior month. Headcount 84, plan 90 - two open sales roles unfilled for 3 months. Rules: max 6 bullets, each decision-relevant, no unexplained jargon, every bullet cites a figure and ends with the "so what" for the board. Do not invent any number or driver not shown above.
Real world

The two open roles that were the real story. A raw AI summary listed all five metrics evenly. The human edit cut three, and led with the buried one: two sales roles unfilled for three months while the order book grew 12% - a revenue risk the board needed to fund now. Same numbers, completely different summary. The judgement about what matters is the human's, every time.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Take last month's real variance commentary (use a synthetic or already-published version if the real one is not cleared for a consumer tool - rail one) and rebuild it with the grounded prompt. Compare the AI draft to what you actually shipped.
  • Save a reusable "house-style" prompt: your structure, your materiality threshold, your terminology, and one pasted example of your best past commentary.
  • Run the exec-summary prompt on a real pack (synthetic numbers if needed) and time your edit pass. Note which tells you keep having to strip - those go into your house prompt as rules.
  • Share one AI-drafted, human-edited write-up with a colleague and ask: "can you tell which parts I edited?" If yes, your edit pass is working.
  • Optional: draft the board-bullet version of the same numbers and see how much further the compression can go.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why feed the model the reconciled numbers and last period's commentary?

The numbers ground the draft so it ties to reality; the example commentary teaches your voice, length, and structure far faster than instructions. Together they cut editing dramatically.

2 · What is the point of a materiality filter in the prompt (e.g. "only variances over $50k or 5%")?

Board narrative should be decision-relevant. A materiality filter forces the draft to focus on the handful of movements that change a decision and drop the symmetry padding.

3 · The AI produced a clean, well-formatted variance commentary. Who owns the final word before it goes to the board?

Rail two. AI drafts; a human reviews, puts the view in, and owns every word that reaches the board. "The AI wrote it" is no defence to a director or an auditor.
