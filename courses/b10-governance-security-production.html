Part 1 · what changes when you go live

Demo to production: the risk stack 7 min live

A prototype only has to work once, for you, on a good day. A production workflow runs unattended, on other people's data, with decisions riding on it - and that opens a stack of risks a demo never had. Know the five that bite finance workflows before you build the controls that stop them.

LiveThe five risks a demo never had4 min▶

Each of these is invisible in a one-off demo and expensive in production. Name them out loud - you cannot control a risk you have not written down.

  • Prompt injection. Hidden instructions inside a document, email, or web page tell the model to ignore your rules, leak data, or take an action. It bites hardest exactly where finance lives now: document Q&A / RAG (session b7) and any agent that reads untrusted content and can act.
  • Data leakage / exfiltration. Confidential financials, client PII, or MNPI end up in a consumer tool, a log, a pasted prompt, or an output shared too widely. The more of a workflow you automate, the more places data can escape.
  • Over-reliance. Once a workflow looks polished, people stop checking. A plausible wrong number ships because "the tool said so" - the exact failure the human gate exists to stop.
  • Model drift / change. The vendor updates the model and your prompts behave differently overnight. A workflow validated in March can quietly regress in April with no code change on your side.
  • No audit trail. Something goes wrong and you cannot reconstruct what the AI was asked, what it returned, or who approved it. Without a trail there is no investigation, no rollback, and no defensible answer for an examiner.
Part 2 · the controls that make it production-grade

The controls 7 min live

Every risk maps to a control you already half-know from the rails in this course. Production is just applying them on purpose, writing them down, and being able to show they are on. Here is the risk-to-control map.

LiveMap each risk to its control4 min▶
RiskThe control that stops it
Data leakageEnterprise tools + hard data boundaries; least-data principle - send only the fields the task needs, never MNPI or client PII into consumer tools.
Over-relianceHuman-in-the-loop gate on every decision; AI drafts and flags, the licensed human reviews, decides, and owns the call.
No audit trailLogging + audit trail: capture the prompt, source, model + version, output, and who approved - for every AI-assisted output.
Prompt injectionInjection awareness: treat retrieved documents and agent inputs as untrusted; constrain tool actions; keep a human gate before anything acts.
Model drift / changeVersion pinning + change management: pin the model where you can, re-run your test set on any model change, log the version used.
Unmanaged sprawlModel inventory (SR 11-7 discipline): list every AI-assisted workflow, its owner, purpose, tier, and review date. What is not inventoried is not governed.
Match the control to the stakes A variance-narrative draft that a human rewrites is low stakes - light controls. A workflow that feeds a covenant test, a credit view, or a filing is high stakes - full inventory entry, review cadence, and a named sign-off. SR 11-7 calls this proportionality: heavier oversight where the decision matters more.
Self-studyReview cadence - governance is not one-and-done2 min read▶

Controls decay. Models change, prompts get edited, the business shifts, and a workflow signed off in Q1 may not be safe by Q3. Set a review cadence proportional to the stakes:

  • High-stakes workflows (feed a decision, a filing, a control): quarterly review at minimum, and an immediate re-check on any model change.
  • Low-stakes assists (draft narrative, formatting, summaries a human rewrites): annual review, or when the tool visibly changes.
  • Every workflow re-runs its test set when the vendor announces a model update - drift is the risk you cannot see coming.
Part 3 · who says yes, and what happens when it breaks

The production checklist + sign-off 5 min live

A control that no one owns is not a control. Going live means three functions sign off, monitoring is on, and there is a written plan for the day it goes wrong. This is exactly the gate leader session a3 asks the business to enforce - here is the builder's version of passing it.

LiveWhat "ready" actually requires3 min▶
  • Three sign-offs. Finance owns accuracy and the business use; risk / model risk owns the tier and review cadence (SR 11-7); IT / security owns the data boundary and access. No single person clears a production workflow alone.
  • Monitoring on. Someone watches outputs and usage after go-live - not to babysit, but to catch drift, misuse, or a spike that signals something changed. A workflow no one looks at is a workflow no one governs.
  • Incident + rollback plan. Written before you need it: how you know something is wrong, who to tell, how to turn the workflow off, and how to revert to the manual process. If you cannot turn it off cleanly, it is not ready.
Real world

The forecast that shipped without a rollback path. A team put an AI-assisted rolling forecast into the monthly cycle - accurate, well-liked, no incident plan. A vendor model update shifted its scenario logic mid-quarter and the numbers drifted. Because there was no version log and no defined revert, the fix took a scramble of two days instead of a clean switch back to the prior spreadsheet. The lesson was cheap only because someone spot-checked. The rollback plan is the control you are gladdest to have never needed.

Demo 1 of 2

Run the readiness checklist against a workflow you built ★ 13 min · everyone builds

No new tool tonight - you audit your own work. Pick ONE workflow you built earlier this course (the b3 close copilot, the b4 forecast, or a b5 report) and score it against each control. The output is a one-page readiness scorecard with gaps and next actions.

Choose the workflow to audit. Write one line: what it does, who would use it, and what decision it feeds. That line sets the stakes - and how heavy the controls need to be.

Score each control Green / Amber / Red: enterprise tool + data boundary, least-data, human-in-loop gate, logging/audit trail, injection awareness, version pinning, inventory entry, review cadence, named owner.

For every Amber or Red, write the single next action that would make it Green - "add a version-logged line to the run", "name the finance sign-off", "stop pasting the full ledger, send only the reconciled lines".

Have the AI help you build the scorecard, then you set the honest scores - the tool does not know your data boundary or who signs off. This is rail two: AI drafts the frame, the human owns the judgement.

Read the Reds out loud. If any high-stakes control is Red, the workflow is not ready to turn on - and now you know exactly why, and what to fix first.

★ The readiness-scorecard promptI built an AI-assisted finance workflow. Help me build a production-readiness scorecard. Here is the workflow: [one line - what it does, who uses it, what decision it feeds]. Make a table with these controls, one row each: enterprise tool + data boundary, least-data, human-in-loop gate, logging / audit trail, prompt-injection awareness, model version pinning, model-inventory entry, review cadence, named owner. Columns: control, what "good" looks like, and a blank score column for me to mark Green / Amber / Red. Do not guess my scores - leave them for me. After the table, list the questions I should ask myself to score each row honestly.
Amber is the honest answer Most real workflows start mostly Amber, and that is fine - Amber means "known gap, named action", not failure. The dangerous score is the one you skipped. A workflow with six honest Ambers and a fix list beats one with nine unexamined Greens.
Demo 2 of 2

Write the incident note + audit-log spec ★ 8 min · build your own

Two short artefacts finish the readiness pack: a plain-language incident/rollback note for the day it breaks, and an audit-log spec that says exactly what to record for every AI-assisted output. Both are things you can hand to IT and risk tomorrow.

Draft the incident note for the same workflow: how you would notice something is wrong, who to alert, how to switch the workflow off, and how to revert to the manual process. Keep it to half a page.

Define the audit-log spec: the fields to capture for every AI-assisted output so the run is reconstructable months later. At minimum - timestamp, user, prompt, source/document, model + version, output, and who reviewed/approved.

Pressure-test the rollback: can you actually turn this off and go back to the prior process cleanly? If the answer is "not really", that is your top Red from Demo 1 - flag it.

Attach both to the scorecard from Demo 1. That three-part pack - scorecard, incident note, log spec - is your production-readiness artefact for this workflow.

★ The incident + audit-log promptFor the same AI-assisted finance workflow, draft two short artefacts. 1. An incident / rollback note (half a page): how we detect a problem, who we alert, how we turn the workflow off, and how we revert to the manual process. 2. An audit-log spec: the exact fields to log for EVERY AI-assisted output so the run can be reconstructed and reviewed later. Start from timestamp, user, prompt, source document, model + version, output, and approver - add any I am missing for a regulated finance context. Keep both plain enough to hand to risk and IT without editing.
Real world

The log line that answered the examiner. A controllership team logged model + version and the approver on every AI-assisted journal narrative - two extra fields, near-zero effort. Months later an internal audit asked "how do you know this commentary was reviewed, and by what model". They pulled the log and answered in minutes. The teams that could not answer that spent weeks reconstructing. The AI did the drafting; the log made it defensible.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What is prompt injection, and where does it bite finance workflows hardest?

Prompt injection is malicious instructions buried in a document, email, or web page that hijack the model. It is most dangerous exactly where finance is heading: RAG over documents and agents that read untrusted content and can take actions.

2 · Why is an audit trail a non-negotiable control?

Logging the prompt, source, model + version, output, and approver is what lets you reconstruct what happened, roll back, and answer an examiner. Without a trail, a problem is unprovable and unfixable.

3 · The AI-assisted workflow produces a number that feeds a decision. Who decides?

The hard rail all course: AI assists, the licensed human decides and owns the decision. The human-in-the-loop gate is the control that stops over-reliance from shipping a plausible wrong answer.
