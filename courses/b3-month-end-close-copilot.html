Part 0

Why the close is the perfect first copilot

Month-end close is repetitive, deadline-bound, and drowning in variance explanations that read the same every month - which is exactly why it is the highest-value, lowest-drama place to put AI to work. Nothing here replaces the accountant; it clears the busywork so judgment gets the time. Tonight you build the copilot in a chat tool, then use a live simulator to feel the one lesson that carries the whole track: a trustworthy answer is grounded and guardrailed, not just well-written.

The close, and where AI actually fits 6 min live

The close is a pipeline: gather the data, reconcile and tie out, analyse the variances, write the commentary, and review before sign-off. AI earns its place in the messy middle - triage, flagging, drafting - while the human owns the two ends: what the numbers are, and whether they are right.

LiveThe three jobs of a close copilot3 min▶

Everything a close copilot does falls into three jobs, each a grounded task you already met in b1 and b2:

  • Reconcile-triage: given two lists (ledger vs bank, sub-ledger vs GL), surface the items that do not tie and rank them by size, so you look at the 10 that matter, not all 400.
  • Anomaly-flag: given actuals vs budget and prior, flag what breaches a materiality threshold or breaks a pattern - and say WHY it was flagged.
  • Variance-narrative: given the reconciled numbers plus business context, draft the "what changed, why, and outlook" commentary in your house style.
Real world

Four days to two. A mid-market controller pointed a copilot at the recurring variance write-ups - the ones that say the same thing every month with new numbers. First drafts that used to eat two days of senior time came back in minutes, leaving the team to do the judgement part: challenge the drivers, chase the odd ones. The close did not get less rigorous. It got faster where rigor was not the point.

Self-studyWhat stays with the human - always2 min read▶

The copilot drafts and triages; it never signs. The non-negotiables, straight from the two rails:

  • The numbers themselves come from your system of record, not the model. AI reasons over figures you supply; it does not source them.
  • Every figure in the commentary is checked against the reconciled data before it goes anywhere near a board pack (remember b1: ungrounded numbers are fiction with formatting).
  • Sign-off is human. "The copilot wrote it" is not an answer to an auditor. You own the words and the numbers.
  • Real data needs a safe tool (rail one): pre-close management figures go in an enterprise or local tool, never a consumer chatbot.
Part 2 · the lever simulator

Drive it yourself: watch trust get built ★ 8 min · everyone plays

Here is the whole lesson of the track in one control panel. The copilot is drafting month-end variance commentary on a small P&L. Every lever you turn on feeds it more context or a tighter guardrail. Turn them on one at a time and watch the commentary go from confident fiction to a checkable, board-grade draft - and flip to the scorecard to watch accuracy climb across a golden set. Notice what never moves the needle: the size of the model.

What the simulator is teaching Start with everything off: the copilot invents numbers, just like an ungrounded chatbot. Add the data and it gets real but flat. Add prior period, business context, and account mapping and it starts to explain and classify. Add guardrails and it finally catches the Marketing overspend and the one-off legal item, and filters the immaterial noise. Grounding and guardrails did that - not a bigger brain.

Build the variance-narrative prompt for real ★ 12 min · everyone builds

Now rebuild what the simulator does, in your own chat tool, on your own small P&L. This is the reusable prompt you will run every close.

Take a small, safe P&L (public numbers or a synthetic 5-line set). Include Budget, Actual, and Prior for each line. Rail one: real pre-close numbers go only in an enterprise or local tool.

Give the copilot a role and the data, then ask for "what changed / why / outlook" commentary - and add every guardrail from the simulator: use only these numbers, show the variance math, apply a materiality threshold, and flag anomalies.

Add business context (a launch, a one-off cost) and watch attribution sharpen - the same jump the "business context" lever makes in the simulator.

Verify: check every figure against your data, confirm the flagged items are the real ones, and edit the wording to your house style. You own the final.

Save the prompt as your reusable "month-end commentary" template. Next month it is a two-minute job.

★ The close-copilot commentary promptYou are a senior management accountant drafting month-end variance commentary. Use ONLY the figures I provide - do not add or estimate any number. Data ($000): Revenue A 4,120 / B 4,000 / P 3,880; COGS A 2,610 / B 2,500 / P 2,380; Marketing A 470 / B 300 / P 290; G&A A 405 / B 400 / P 395; Legal A 180 / B 0 / P 0. Write commentary as: (1) what changed vs budget and vs prior, showing the variance math; (2) why, using this context - Marketing reflects the Q2 launch campaign, Legal is a one-off settlement; (3) a one-line outlook. Rules: flag any line more than 15% OR 100 over budget as an anomaly to investigate, note the Legal item as an unbudgeted one-off below the line, treat variances under the threshold as immaterial, and if anything needed is missing say "not in the data".
Notice the overlap That prompt is the simulator with every lever on: data, prior, context, mapping (below-the-line), and guardrails (threshold + flag + "not in the data"). The simulator is just this prompt made clickable.

Reconcile-triage: find the 10 that matter ★ 8 min · build your own

The other everyday close job: two lists that should agree but do not. The copilot triages; you investigate.

Make two short synthetic lists that should tie - e.g. a sub-ledger and a GL balance per account, with a few deliberate mismatches (a timing difference, a transposition, a missing entry).

Ask the copilot to compare them, list only the items that do NOT reconcile, rank by absolute difference, and suggest the likely reason for each (timing, transposition, missing) - as a hypothesis to check, not a conclusion.

Add the guardrail: "show the arithmetic for each difference and say 'cannot determine' rather than guessing a cause you cannot support".

Investigate the top items yourself. The copilot narrowed 400 lines to the handful that matter; the judgement call on each is still yours.

★ The reconcile-triage promptHere are two lists that should agree: [SUB-LEDGER] and [GL]. Compare them and return ONLY the accounts that do not reconcile, as a table: account, sub-ledger, GL, difference (show the subtraction), and a likely-cause hypothesis (timing / transposition / missing entry) to investigate. Rank by absolute difference, largest first. If you cannot support a cause, say "cannot determine". Do not invent accounts or balances not in the lists.
Real world

The transposition that hid for three months. A team fed their intercompany recs to a copilot as a triage step. It ranked a 540 vs 450 difference to the top with "possible transposition" - a digit swap that manual eyes had skated past two closes running. AI did not fix it; it just put it where a human would finally look. That is the whole value: attention, aimed.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Run the variance-narrative prompt on a real (safe) month, then do a strict verify pass - tick every number against source. Note anything the draft got wrong; that is your reminder to always check.
  • Play the simulator again and predict the quality score before you toggle each lever. Being able to predict it means you understand what drives trust.
  • Adapt the reconcile-triage prompt to one real reconciliation you own. Keep it as a monthly template.
  • Draft a two-line "human sign-off" note you would attach to any AI-assisted commentary: what you checked, and that you own it.
  • Optional: time how long the commentary took this month vs last. Bring the number to session b5, where we go deeper on board-grade narrative.

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the simulator, what finally makes the copilot catch the Marketing overspend and the one-off legal item?

Grounding plus guardrails catch anomalies; model size does not. That is the lesson the whole track is built on.

2 · Which parts of the close does the copilot own?

AI assists the messy middle; a human owns what the numbers are and whether they are right. That is rail two in practice.

3 · Your variance prompt should always include which guardrail?

Grounding plus "show your work" plus "admit gaps" is what makes the draft checkable - and keeps invented figures out of the board pack.
